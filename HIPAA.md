# Breach Notification Rule 
The passage of the HIT ECH Act introduced the Breach Notification Rule. Under this rule, covered 
entities are required to send a first-class notification letter to individuals affected by a PHI 
breach within 60 days of when the breach was discovered. Notification can be made by 
electronic mail if specified as a preference by the individual. 
Regardless of the notification method, notice of a breach must include: 
• A brief description of what happened, including the date of the breach and the date of the 
discovery of the breach, if known. 
• A description of the types of unsecured protected health information that were involved in the 
breach. 
• The steps individuals should take to protect themselves from potential harm resulting from 
the breach. 
• A brief description of what the covered entity involved is doing to investigate the breach, to 
mitigate losses, and to protect against any further breaches. 
• Contact procedures for individuals to ask questions or learn additional information, which 
shall include a toll-free telephone number, an email address, website, or postal address. 
A covered entity must provide notice of a breach affecting 500 or more individuals to the HHS 
within 60 days of discovering the breach. If a breach affects 500 or more individuals of a 
particular state or jurisdiction, the covered entity must also notify local media outlets within 60 
days. Where a breach affects fewer than 500 records, covered entities must notify the HHS 
within 60 days of the end of the calendar year. Covered entities are also required to maintain a 
log of all data security breaches that must be submitted to the HHS annually. 


# Risk Assessment 
However, a breach notification is not required if the healthcare institution determines that there 
is a low chance the PHI has been compromised. To make this decision, the healthcare institution 
must conduct a risk assessment that considers the following four factors: 
1. The kind of PHI involved and the amount, including the types of identifiers and how likely it is 
that the patient can be re-identified, 
2. The unauthorized person who used the PHI or to whom the PHI was disclosed, 
3. Whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed, and 
4. The lengths taken to mitigate the risks to the PHI. 

# Tier 1 
A Tier 1 violation occurs when the covered entity or business associate did not know and would not have known that they had violated the HIPAA Rules, even if they had 
conducted due diligence. 
• Level of culpability: No knowledge 
• Minimum penalty per violation: $127 
• Maximum penalty per violation: $63,793 
• Maximum annual penalty: $1,919,173 

# Tier 2 
A Tier 2 violation occurs when the HIPAA Rulesl breach was due to reasonable cause and was not because of willful neglect. 
• Level of culpability: Reasonable cause 
• Minimum penalty per violation: $1,280 
• Maximum penalty per violation: $63,973 
• Maximum annual penalty: $1,919,173 

# Tier 3 
A Tier 3 violation occurs when the violation was the result of willful neglect but was corrected during the 30-day period beginning on the first date the covered entity or 
business associate liable for the penalty knew, or, by conducting reasonable due diligence, would have known that the violation had occurred. 
• Level of culpability: Willful neglect — corrective action taken 
• Minimum penalty per violation: $12,794 
• Maximum penalty per violation: $63,973 
• Maximum annual penalty: $1,919,173 

# Tier 4 
A Tier 4 violation occurs when the violation was the result of willful neglect but was not corrected during the 30-day period beginning on the first date the covered entity 
or business associate liable for the penalty knew, or, by conducting reasonable due diligence, would have known that the violation had occurred. 
• Level of culpability: Willful neglect — no corrective action taken 
• Minimum penalty per violation: $63,973 
• Maximum penalty per violation: $1,919,173 
• Maximum annual penalty: $1,919,173 


# Criminal Penalties 
Fines aren't the only penalty that can be issued for violations of the HIPAA Rules. In cases where a person knowingly uses, obtains, or discloses PHI, criminal 
prosecutions can apply, with a prison sentence of up to 10 years. One such case occurred in 2018 when a doctor was convicted and sentenced to one year in prison and 
received a $50,000 fine for giving patient information to a pharmaceutical company.
